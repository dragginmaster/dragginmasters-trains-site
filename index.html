Identify Your Train

No box? No problem. Here's how to pin down the make, model, and manufacturer of a mystery piece.

1. Photograph it — including the bottom

Top, both sides, and the underside. You'll need the bottom shot. That's where the answer usually lives.

2. Google Lens the side profile

This solves a lot of cases outright — Lens will usually name the prototype, like an EMD F7 or a 4-6-2 Pacific. But here's the catch: several manufacturers made the same units, and a photo of the shell can't always tell a Kato from a Model Power. That's what the rest of this page is for.

3. Flip it over and read the underside

Look for a brand name, country of origin, or catalog number molded or stamped into the fuel tank, frame, or truck sideframes. Most of the time, this ends the mystery right here.

4. Match the manufacturer tells

Same shell, different maker — here's how to tell them apart. Markings vary by era, so treat these as "look for," not gospel.

ScaleManufacturerWhat to look for
NKato"KATO" stamped on the fuel tank underside or chassis; often "Made in Japan." Blackened metal wheels and crisp printing. Note: Atlas N from the 80s–90s often runs on Kato mechanisms — check for KATO stamps under an Atlas shell.
NMicro-Trains"Micro-Trains" or "Made in U.S.A." on the underside; distinctive operating knuckle couplers.
NBachmannBrand name on the underside; modern production usually marked "Made in China."
NModel PowerOften Italian-made (Rivarossi heritage); older tooling, simpler detail, shells frequently unmarked.
NMinitrix / Arnold / Fleischmann / RocoEuropean makers — look for "Made in Germany" or "Made in Austria."
HOAthearn"Athearn" stamps on the frame; older blue-box era often "Made in U.S.A." Blue-box diesels have 4 holes in the fuel tank for the motor mounts — a dead giveaway even when the stamps are worn off.
HOWalthers / Life-Like (Proto)"Walthers," "Life-Like," or "Proto 2000" markings on the chassis.
HOBachmannSame as N — brand name on the underside.
HOBroadway Limited"Broadway Limited" or "BLI" branding, usually on the chassis.

5. No markings at all?

  • Couplers: the big Rapido loop means older or budget N scale; working knuckle couplers mean newer or upgraded.
  • Wheels: blackened metal wheels point to quality makers; shiny or plastic wheels point to budget lines.
  • Pop the shell: usually one screw. The chassis and motor are often stamped even when the shell isn't.
  • Weight: a die-cast metal chassis usually means an older premium mechanism; all-plastic often means budget.

6. Still stuck?

Search the road name plus the road number plus the wheel arrangement — e.g. "Santa Fe 2341 4-axle diesel N scale." Road numbers are often specific enough to nail it.

Why this matters for value: the manufacturer sets the tier — a Kato F7 and a Model Power F7 are different money. Once you've got the make, look it up in the Price Guide tab.

Sell it yourself: more money, more work

  • eBay — the best return for most collections, sold piece by piece. Slow, and you're doing the photos, listings, and shipping.
  • Facebook Marketplace / Craigslist — good for local, no shipping. Expect hagglers; works best for smaller lots.
  • Train shows & swap meets — rent a table and sell direct. The Great Midwest Train Show in Wheaton, IL runs monthly with 300+ tables.
  • Local hobby shops — ask about consignment or outright purchase. Even if they don't buy, dealers usually know who does.
Set expectations: dealers buy wholesale — they have to resell for a profit, so cash offers come in under retail. That's normal, not a scam. Selling piece by piece yourself almost always nets more; selling to a dealer is faster and zero hassle. Pick your poison.
